The job, and what to type

Use only notes you wrote yourself, in your own words. Not textbook pages, lecturer slides or handouts, question papers, a classmate's notes, or notes copied word for word. The notes below are a sample on rates of reaction, made up for this demo.

Turn my chemistry notes below into the 5 key points I most need for my test tomorrow.
- Plain words. Explain any term the first time you use it.
- Then list everything from my notes you left out of the 5 points, so I know what I still need to revise.

My notes:
Rates of reaction
1. Rate = how fast reactants turn into products. Measure it as gas made per second, or mass lost per second.
2. Particles only react when they collide with enough energy. That minimum energy is the activation energy.
3. Hotter: particles move faster, so they collide more often and more collisions have enough energy. Rule of thumb: about twice as fast for every 10 °C rise (not exact).
4. More concentrated solution (or higher pressure for a gas): more particles in the same space, so more collisions, so faster.
5. Smaller pieces or a powder: more surface area, so faster. Fine powders can be dangerous: flour dust can explode.
6. Catalyst: speeds up the reaction, is not used up, lowers the activation energy.
7. Exam trap: a catalyst does NOT give more product. Same amount, just sooner.
8. Measuring: collect the gas in a gas syringe, or stand the flask on a balance with cotton wool in its neck.
9. Graph of product against time: steep at the start, then levels off when one reactant runs out. Steeper line = faster.
10. Safety: wear goggles; acid is corrosive.

Why these lines

"The 5 key points I most need for my test tomorrow" gives it a number and a deadline, so it has to choose instead of rewriting everything. "Plain words. But note 9 (the graph levels off when one reactant runs out) and note 10 (wear goggles, acid is corrosive) are both already in point 5, a few lines above the table. So of the ten notes, only two were really left out: note 5 (fine powders can be dangerous, flour dust can explode) and note 8 (the cotton wool in the flask's neck). Both were on the list, so nothing went missing. But a list that mixes what's still left with what's already covered can't tell you what's still left. You'd go back over the safe parts and never know how little was missing.

Chemistry: Rates of Reaction — 5 Key Points
Test tomorrow · Priority revision

These are the 5 most important things to remember. Learn these first!

The 5 key points
1. What is the rate of reaction?

The rate of reaction means how fast reactants (starting substances) turn into products (new substances).

It can be measured by the gas produced per second.

It can also be measured by the mass lost per second.

Remember: Rate = speed of a chemical reaction.

2. Particles must collide with enough energy

Particles only react when they collide with enough energy.

This minimum energy is called activation energy (the minimum energy needed for a reaction to happen).

More successful collisions = faster reaction.

Collisions without enough energy do not cause a reaction.

3. Three ways to make a reaction faster

Higher temperature: Particles move faster, collide more often and have more energy.

Higher concentration or pressure: More particles are packed into the same space, causing more collisions.

Smaller pieces or powder: More surface area (more of the substance exposed), allowing more collisions.

Important exam fact: A 10°C temperature increase makes a reaction roughly twice as fast. This is a rule of thumb, not an exact rule.

4. Catalysts speed up reactions

A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up.

It lowers the activation energy.

It makes the reaction faster.

It does NOT increase the total amount of product made.

Learn more
Common exam trap

A catalyst makes the same amount of product in less time. It does not make extra product!

5. Measuring and understanding reaction rates

A gas syringe collects gas to measure how much is produced.

A balance measures mass loss as gas escapes.

A product-against-time graph starts steep and then levels off when one reactant runs out.

A steeper graph means a faster reaction.

Safety: Wear goggles when doing experiments. Acid is corrosive (it can damage skin and other materials).
